MODEL RAILWAYS — Telephones and the Railway
============================================
File: /history/model_railways.mp3
Estimated duration: ~50 seconds

--- SCRIPT ---

The telephone and the railway grew up together. Both transformed Victorian Britain, and both relied on the same thing — a network of wires stretching across the country.

Railway stations were among the first public places to have telephone boxes installed. Travellers could call ahead to arrange a taxi, confirm their arrival, or let someone know their train was running late — a common enough occurrence then, as now.

The railways also had their own private telephone network. Signal boxes, station offices, and goods yards were all connected, allowing signalmen to coordinate the safe movement of trains along the line. Before radio, the telephone was the only way to communicate between signal boxes, and a missed call could have serious consequences.

The red telephone box became as much a part of the railway station as the platform bench and the departures board. You'd find one on most station forecourts, often standing beside a post box — another piece of iconic British street furniture in the same distinctive red.

Next time you see a telephone box near a station, you'll know it's carrying on a tradition that goes back well over a hundred years.
